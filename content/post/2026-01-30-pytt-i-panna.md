## Intro
For my American readers, husmanskost literally translates to "home cooking", but it's perhaps better known as "everyman's cuisine": the staples seen in most Swedish family meals, schools, and cafeterias alike. Coming from a country without a distinct food identity, it has been a joy to cook and sample some of the traditional meals which have been passed down and remade for generations within the Nordic countries. Not all of the dishes quite match my softened American palete, but it has been fun nonetheless to work my way through elk stews, meatballs, and blood puddings (yikes). 

Pytt i panna, or Peter Pan as it is better known in our Swenglish household, is the quintessential husmanskost dish. In this recipe, I cook it entirely from scratch, but traditionally you use any leftover meat in the fridge combined with finely diced potatoes and onions to a single skillet, typically served with a fried egg and pickled beets. Peter Pan falls under my own category of "barnmat", or child food, that Ida resorts to when eating the occasional "girl dinner". Its uniform in texture and mild flavor are about as neutral as it gets, but it reliably hits the spot when you need a fast meal, and adulting is too hard sometimes.

Typically it comes in these large frozen bags:

![Frozen Pytt](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/frozen_pytt.jpeg)

I don't know how much pytt i panna the average couple consumes, but we can take one of these bags down in a week. It's very possible my elevated pytt i panna consumption is the most Swedish thing I do on a weekly basis. 

Anyways, here is my take on pytt i panna, inspired by the lovely husmanskost cookbook gifted to me for Christmas (tack Paula!).

## Ingredients
- `1.5 lbs` pork chops or other meat, diced (~750 g)
- `1 pack` bacon (~150 g)
- `1.5 lbs` potatoes (~750 g)
- `2` yellow onions
- `4 cloves` garlic
- `2-3` eggs
- `3 tbsp` butter (~45 g)
- `1 tbsp` beef fond/bouillon (~15 mL)
- `1/2 cup` water (~100 mL)
- `1 tbsp` salt (to taste)
- `1 tsp` black pepper
- Fresh herbs (parsley or similar, for garnish)
- Optional: dried oregano

![Ingredient Spread](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/pytt_ingredients.jpeg) 

Like I mentioned before, any meat can be used for this. I selected pork chops because they were on sale, but you can just as easily use sausage, beef, etc. and still have it pass as pytt i panna.

***Tip:*** Use large potatoes! It is a nightmare peeling and dicing tiny potatoes.

<font color="red">***WARNING:***</font> Do not attempt this dish without a dicing machine. The copious amount of cutting effort is not worth the payoff.

## Directions
The first and largest hurdle to overcome is the cutting prep work. The coarseness of the meat/veggie cubes is up to your own discretion, but I chose about 1/4" (~6mm), which required some Michelin-level attention to detail to maintain across all of the ingredients.

Start by peeling your potatoes. I was lured in by the "gourmet potatis" label, but in reality these potatoes were way too small for this dish and it cost me a few fingertips attempting to peel them.

![Peeled Potatoes](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/peeled_potatoes.jpeg)

Next, dice the potatoes up into your desired size. The more uniformity you have, the better resulting mouthfeel, but it is quite painstaking to achieve this by hand.

![Cut Potatoes](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/cut_potatoes.jpeg)

I did not photograph it, but we also need to cut up the bacon now as well. The thickness does not matter, just cut into small enough pieces to fry quickly.

Massage your wrists out and keep on dicing the onions around the same coarseness, and mince the garlic. Finish all of the dicing with the meat. If using pork, be sure to cut off a lot (or all of) the fat, since we will be using bacon and garlic in the dish as well, and it can easily become too much.

![Diced Ingredients](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/diced_ingredients.jpeg)

***Tip:*** Save and rinse your onion and garlic peels in a Ziploc bag, and store in the freezer. I will soon be showing how to make a homemade vegetable stock using kitchen scraps.

![Onion Scraps](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/onion_scraps.jpeg)

Woohoo! All the dicing done! Time to cook!

Add the bacon to a non-stick frying pan and cook on medium-high until crispy. Remove and drain onto a paper towel. Add the potatoes directly into the bacon grease and begin frying until they turn golden brown.

![Potato Pan](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/potato_pan.jpeg)

***Note:*** My LeCreuset pot did not like frying these potatoes, which is why I recommend using a frypan to do it more easily.

When the potatoes are done, move them into a bowl and set aside. Melt 2 tablespoons of butter into the pan, and then stir in the garlic and onions.

![Potato Pan](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/onion_pan.jpeg)

Once the onions are translucent and soft, add in the meat. Cook the meat until it loses its color (if using pork), and reaches a safe temperature. When done, mix the potatoes back into the frypan.

![All in Pan](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/all_in_pan.jpeg)

Add the bacon in as well, and salt and pepper to taste. Use less than 1/2 cup (~100 mL) of water and a healthy dash of the beef fond to add some flavoring to the pot. Be careful not to overdo the water and make the pot soggy. If you have oregano on hand, this can be a nice addition as well.

![Done Pan](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/done_pan.jpeg)

Turn off the heat and let it rest. In another nonstick pan, melt more butter and fry an egg(s) to your liking.

![Fried Egg](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/fried_egg.jpeg)

When the egg is done, plate the pytt i panna with some fresh parsley, fried egg, and beetroot. Here is how mine turned out:

![Final Plate](/img/cooking/pytt-i-panna/final_pytt.jpeg)

### - Summary
I am positive that any native Swede would laugh if I told them it took me 2 hours to cook pytt i panna. It was *excruciating* to stand and dice ingredients for 1.5 hours, for a fairly trivial dish. I was pleased enough with how it turned out, as it was quite tasty, but the trade-off for the effort was not even remotely close. For Ida, it "did not beat the frozen one" and "lacked some color". The pork was probably not the best visual choice for the dish, as it looked a little bit ghoulish once the pink drained out of it. To give a rough taste comparison, it was a cross of camp-fire dinner and a Denny's breakfast skillet.

Husmanskost nearly broke me this time, but I will be back with more traditional flavors from the heart of Scandinavia.
